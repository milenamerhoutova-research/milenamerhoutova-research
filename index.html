The question

Resilience is more than “bouncing back.”

My doctoral research asks how psychological resilience is constructed through processes of meaning-making and narrative interpretation following significant adversity.

I am interested in what happens between an experience and the meaning we eventually give it. People may revisit an event, tell the story differently, find new words for it, connect it with earlier experiences, or place it within a larger personal or cultural story. Those changes in interpretation can become part of how people understand themselves and their capacity to move forward.

“Your story is not only what happened. It is also the meaning you make of what happened.”

Current Montréal research

Listening to stories of change.

During my visiting research period at Concordia University, I am exploring these questions in the context of the Jewish diaspora in Greater Montréal.

The current study focuses on the experience of significant romantic relationship dissolution. I am interested in participants’ own narratives: how they describe what happened, how their understanding may have changed, and how they make sense of themselves, their relationships, communities and the future afterwards.

Who?Jewish diaspora residents of Greater Montréal who meet the study criteria.

Why stories?

Every person makes meaning differently.

There is no “correct” way to understand a breakup, adversity, or recovery.

That is precisely why individual narratives matter. Rather than reducing an experience to a score or a checklist, this research pays attention to the language through which people describe their experiences and the interpretations they develop over time.

The project is informed by phenomenological, interpretive, narrative and semiotic approaches. In other words: I want to understand the experience as it is lived, while also paying attention to how meaning is communicated and constructed through signs, words and stories.
